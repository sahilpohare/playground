# Ops jobs

Playground's background work runs as ops jobs: the Dashboard's supervisor, the sweeps, the merge train, the usage reads and the rest.
Each job is a setting, `jobs.<name>` (`ops-merge-train` is `jobs.opsMergeTrain`), whose value is the job: whether it is on, its schedule, its command or launchd program, and why it is paused.
A stored value holds only the fields it changes, and reads over the job's entry in Playground's registry, so a job turned on still runs the command and schedule Playground ships, including after an update changes them.
A fresh install has every job off but two: `jobs.opsDashboard`, the Dashboard's own, which serves first run's checks and every settings write, and `jobs.opsUsage`, which reads your Claude login's 5-hour and week usage every 5 minutes, so the Dashboard shows them with no setting.
On a Mac a third is on too, `jobs.opsKeepAwake`, which keeps an idle Mac awake ([below](#keep-an-idle-mac-awake)).

## Turn a job on

Settings > Jobs on the Dashboard lists every job, an org's own beside Playground's, with what it does, when it runs, whether it is on and whether your settings or an org's set it.
Its switch saves the job's `enabled` field alone at the job's scope, your login's for Playground's job and its org's for an org's own, with a reason, over any other field your scope's value holds.

A session cannot write a job's setting (the `no-extensions-write` hook refuses `playground settings set` and `reset` of every `jobs.<name>`, and `settings import-jobs` but its `--dry-run`), so it asks you with an Inbox guard decision whose value is `{"enabled": true}` or `{"enabled": false}` alone, or a new `{"config": {...}}` alone, or for an org's own job its whole row ([below](#an-orgs-own-job)); your Approve writes it the same way, an `enabled` or a `config` over the row its scope holds ([Inbox](/docs/inbox)).
The one exception is how often a job runs: the Ops lead may set a job's row whose only change from the row your scope stores is `schedule.every`, its interval, where the job already runs on one and no other scope stores a row for it.
It does so with the `playground settings set` call alone on its line, since a variable set beside it could move the write to another scope.

From your own terminal, set your scope's value, which replaces any value it held, with a reason:

```bash
playground settings set jobs.opsSweep '{"enabled": true}' --reason "<why>" [--scope <scope>]
```

Your login's value wins over your org's, as for every setting, so a job you turn on or off holds whatever your org set; a field your value leaves out reads Playground's, not your org's.
Moving a schedule or pausing a job with `pausedFor` is the same kind of write, naming that field.

## Change a job's config

A job with a `config`, the settings of its own it reads on each pass, shows a card under its row in Settings > Jobs: the config as JSON in a box you edit, and Save, which writes the new object at the job's scope at once, over the rest of the value there.
Playground checks only that it is a JSON object; the job checks each field itself, and when it refuses one it writes why as `configRefused` in its heartbeat, which the card shows in red under the box until a beat holds none.

## Reach a Dashboard on another machine

When the Dashboard runs on another machine, such as a DGX Spark, it listens on that machine's loopback alone, and the `ops-tunnel` job on your Mac brings it to you.
The job holds an ssh tunnel with the key `~/.ssh/playground_spark` from a port on your Mac's loopback to the same port on the other machine's loopback, 1355 by default, so the link is the address of that machine's live Dashboard, `https://dashboard.playground.localhost:1355/dashboard`, and nothing listens off loopback at either end.
The link's host comes from the `live.branch` setting (`live/dashboard` by default), and the Dashboard's API, `dashboard.api.playground.localhost`, goes through the same port.
Its config names the machine: `{"host": "<name or address>", "port": 1355}`, with optional `key` and `user` (your Mac's user by default).
That machine serves its names under the certificate authority your Mac already trusts, since its setup copies your Mac's portless folder there, so there is no trust step.
Two Approves set the job up, in this order: a session posts the host as an Inbox guard decision, `{"config": {"host": ...}}`, then the switch, `{"enabled": true}`, and you click Approve on each.
If your Mac does not trust the certificate that machine serves, `playground doctor` says so and the job posts no link.

Once the tunnel is up and your Mac trusts the certificate the other machine serves, the job posts the link to your Inbox once: click the card's title and the Dashboard opens in a new tab.
That Dashboard keeps a sign-in of its own, apart from your Mac's, since a stack on a port other than 443 names its sign-in cookie by its port.
After a drop, the job starts ssh again within 2 seconds, waiting up to a minute between tries while it keeps failing.
`playground doctor` shows a `tunnel` line while the job is on: ok with the link, or why it cannot connect, such as a key the other machine refused, a host key this Mac does not know yet, the port already taken on your Mac, or a certificate your Mac does not trust yet.

## Carry messages to another machine

Sessions on one machine message each other directly, and a session on another machine, such as a probe started there over ssh, sends with `playground send <name> <text>` ([CLI](/docs/cli#message-a-session-on-another-machine-playground-send)).
The `ops-peer-relay` job carries those lines across: it runs on one machine, holds one ssh connection to each machine `PLAYGROUND_PEERS` names in the Dashboard's `.env`, the same setting the System page reads, with the key `PLAYGROUND_PEER_KEY` names (`~/.ssh/playground_spark` by default), and copies each new line both ways, once each.
On the other machine it runs a small script from that machine's own Playground checkout, at the same path, so nothing is installed there, and that checkout must be at a release that has the script, or the job says so; it also says when the other machine stays silent or ends before the script answers.
Lines carry from the job's first connection on, so a line written before it stays where it was written.
After a drop it starts ssh again within 2 seconds, waiting up to a minute between tries, tells Ops once, and a line written meanwhile still arrives once, keeping the time it was sent.

## Reach the model servers on another machine

The `ops-model-tunnel` job brings the peer's local model servers, which [`playground model use`](/docs/cli#start-a-local-model-playground-model-use) starts on its loopback, to this machine's loopback: one ssh connection forwards `127.0.0.1:18000` here to the peer's `127.0.0.1:8000`, and `18001` to `8001`, with nothing listening off loopback at either end.
The peer is the one `localModels.machine` names in `PLAYGROUND_PEERS`, or the only one it names, reached with the key `PLAYGROUND_PEER_KEY` names (`~/.ssh/playground_spark` by default).
After a drop it starts ssh again within 2 seconds, waiting up to a minute between tries while it keeps failing.

## Keep an idle Mac awake

The `ops-keep-awake` job keeps an idle Mac from sleeping, so the ops jobs and the WhatsApp link run on while you are away and the display is off.
It holds `caffeinate -i -s` as its one child: the display still sleeps and the screen still locks, it needs no `sudo`, and it sets no `pmset` value.
It runs on macOS alone: on any other platform the runner leaves it out of `jobs.json`, so there it is no job.
It is on by default on a Mac, and `pmset -g assertions` lists its hold; turn it off as any job, with `jobs.opsKeepAwake` set to `{"enabled": false}` ([above](#turn-a-job-on)).

## Restore now

The ops-restore row in Settings > Jobs holds Restore now, your way back when the main checkout's guards are gone and no session can repair it, with the job on or off.
It runs ops-restore's own steps once, from the Dashboard's server: it puts the checkout's files back, installs, runs `link-skills.sh`, and checks the guards again.
The Dashboard runs from its own checkout, so the page still works while the main checkout is broken.
Under the button it says what it restored, that every guard is in place so nothing was, or the step it stopped at; ops and the dispatcher get the same words.

## How a change reaches the runner

At the top of every pass, the ops runner (`scripts/ops/up.py`) renders the jobs from your extensions into `<data>/ops/jobs.json` and writes a plist for each launchd job that is on into `<data>/ops/launch-agents`.
A machine with no extensions keeps its own `jobs.json`.
A plist whose job is now off moves to the trash under the `jobs` carve-out ([CLI](/docs/cli)), and its installed copy under `jobs-installed`, as below; one the trash refuses stays, and the runner writes why to `<data>/ops/up.log`.
When that render cannot be trusted, the runner keeps the last `jobs.json` and writes why to `<data>/ops/up.log`.
A job the registry does not know, such as one of your own scripts, stays in `jobs.json` as it is, unless an org's own job of its name ([below](#an-orgs-own-job)) replaces it.
A job Playground no longer ships, such as `ops-awake`, stays in `jobs.json` turned off, so the runner stops it, and the Scripts list shows no row for it; a setting of it you still hold clears with `playground settings reset jobs.<camelName>`, `jobs.opsAwake` for `ops-awake`.
Each `script` item an org holds as a folder is installed into `<data>/ops/<key>/` in the same pass, beside the state its job keeps there, which the render never touches.
The folder's `.revision` names the accepted revision that last wrote it, and a job running from the folder restarts onto a new one at its next idle moment, never in the middle of a pass, logging the revision it picked up; a job that runs a skill's script from the extensions folder restarts the same way on accepted revisions of that skill or of any other its scripts import.
A job's command names such a script by its place in your extensions, `ext:<scope>/<path>` (`python3 -I ext:<org>/skills/vault/scripts/vault.py run`), in place of a folder path: the runner turns each `ext:` word into the path `bun packages/scripts/src/playground/rendered.ts <scope>/<path>` prints, at each start, so the job runs the latest accepted revision with no row to repoint.
A start whose `ext:` word your extensions lack opens no pane, and the runner writes the reader's refusal to `<data>/ops/up.log`.

launchd reads `~/Library/LaunchAgents`, not `<data>/ops/launch-agents`, so the same pass installs each launchd job you turn on: while launchd has not loaded it, its rendered plist is copied into `~/Library/LaunchAgents` when none is there and loaded with `launchctl bootstrap`, tried again on the next pass when launchd refuses, and a plist already there stays as it is.
Only Playground's own labels (`com.playground.*`) are installed or removed, so no other app's agent is touched.
A launchd job you turn off has its plist in `~/Library/LaunchAgents` moved to the trash under the `jobs-installed` carve-out, beside the `jobs` one above, and is unloaded with `launchctl bootout` while launchd still holds it, tried again on the next pass when launchd refuses.
A launchd job still down after 3 passes in a row is told to Ops once, and again only after it has run healthy in between.

### On Linux

Linux has no launchd, so on Linux the same pass runs each launchd job as a systemd user unit of the same name, `<label>.service`:

| Job                  | launchd label                       | systemd user unit                           |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| `ops-flow`           | `com.playground.ops-flow`           | `com.playground.ops-flow.service`           |
| `ops-herdr`          | `com.playground.ops-herdr`          | `com.playground.ops-herdr.service`          |
| `ops-restore`        | `com.playground.ops-restore`        | `com.playground.ops-restore.service`        |
| `ops-session-resume` | `com.playground.ops-session-resume` | `com.playground.ops-session-resume.service` |
| `ops-tick`           | `com.playground.ops-tick`           | `com.playground.ops-tick.service`           |
| `ops-zai-usage`      | `com.playground.ops-zai-usage`      | `com.playground.ops-zai-usage.service`      |

A unit runs its plist's program from the same folder and logs to the same `launchd.log`, with `~/.bun/bin` first on its `PATH`, and systemd restarts it always, 10 seconds apart, as launchd keeps a job alive.
A stop ends the job's own process alone (`KillMode=process`), so the herdr servers ops-herdr starts outlive it, as they outlive its launchd job.
While systemd does not run it, its unit is written into `~/.config/systemd/user` when none is there, its log's folder is made, and it is started with `systemctl --user enable --now`, tried again on the next pass when systemd refuses, and a unit already there stays as it is.
A job you turn off is disabled with `systemctl --user disable`, its unit moved to the trash under the `jobs-units` carve-out ([CLI](/docs/cli)) and systemd reloaded so it forgets the unit, and then stopped while it runs, the unit going first since ops-tick's own pass runs the render.
Only Playground's own units (`com.playground.*.service`) are installed or removed, so no other user unit is touched.
`playground doctor` checks each one with `systemctl --user is-active`, and `playground uninstall` disables and removes it.

A user's units stop when that user logs out unless lingering is on, so a pass that starts a unit turns it on once with `loginctl enable-linger <uid>`, which asks for no password, and says so in `<data>/ops/up.log`; when the machine refuses it, that log names the refusal, the units still run while you are logged in, and `playground doctor`'s `linger` line names the command.
Each `systemctl --user` call finds your session's bus in `/run/user/<uid>` when `XDG_RUNTIME_DIR` is unset, as in a terminal pane, a cron job or ssh.

## Bring an existing jobs.json in

A machine that ran its jobs from a hand-kept `jobs.json` brings them into settings once, at the switch:

```bash
playground settings import-jobs <jobs.json> [--launch-agents <dir>] [--scope <scope>] [--dry-run]
```

Each job the registry knows becomes its `jobs.<name>` setting at your login's scope (or `--scope`), on unless `jobs.json` disabled it, with a launchd job's program and folder read from its plist in `--launch-agents` (`~/Library/LaunchAgents` by default).
`--dry-run` prints what it would set and writes nothing.
You run it yourself: a session's run is refused but for `--dry-run`, since it would turn jobs on.
Every other job is listed as kept, and stays in `jobs.json`.

## Bring your own scripts in

A script your jobs run from a folder of its own (a runner, its helpers and the config it reads) becomes a `script` of your org once, at the switch:

```bash
playground ext import-scripts <ops-folder> <job>... --org <org> [--dry-run]
```

Each folder named becomes `<org>/scripts/<job>/` in your extensions: its top-level `.py`, `.sh` and `.json` files, but `state.json` and `watch.json`.
The run prints everything it leaves in the folder by name, so a dry run shows what stays behind.
A change to the script or its config is then a new revision, put with `playground ext put`, and the next pass installs it.

## An org's own job

An org runs a job of its own, one the registry does not know, by shipping its script and its row together in its extensions:

- the script as `<org>/scripts/<key>/`, which each pass installs into `<data>/ops/<key>/`;
- the row as `jobs.<camelName>` in `<org>/settings.json`, with every field a job has: `callsModel`, `command`, `description`, `enabled`, `heartbeat` and `schedule`, and `cwd` and `config` when it needs them.

Its `description`, which every row needs, is the line the Dashboard shows for what the job does, since core names no org's job.

You place the row, turn the job on or off and change its `config` with `playground settings set jobs.<camelName> '<row>' --scope <org> --reason "<why>"`, or by approving a guard decision that carries the row, or its `enabled` or `config` alone over the row the org holds; Settings > Jobs' switch and config card change the job in place, and take it out with `playground settings reset jobs.<camelName> --scope <org>`.
The write checks the row as the render reads it (below), against the script folders that org's extensions hold at the time, and refuses it at your login's scope or when another org already adds the job.

```json
{
  "jobs.opsWatch": {
    "callsModel": false,
    "command": "{bun} --no-env-file {data}/ops/ops-watch/cli.ts --data {data}",
    "config": { "prefixes": ["INC"] },
    "description": "Watches the org's own queue.",
    "enabled": true,
    "heartbeat": "ops-watch",
    "schedule": { "always": true }
  }
}
```

The render takes the row only when it runs in a terminal, stays in its org's own script folder and keeps to its own name:

- its `command` is `{bun}`, then `--no-env-file` if it wants it, then a script in a folder that org ships, `{data}/ops/<key>/<file>`, then plain words or `{data}`;
- its `cwd` is that folder or one under it, and that folder when the row leaves it out;
- its `heartbeat` is the job's own name, and it names no `stop` group, since ops stops a job's group before each start.

Any other row is refused: one off that shape, one naming `launchd` or `program`, a second org's row for a job the first adds (scopes in A to Z order), and a row for such a job at your login's scope.
Each refusal is a `jobs: refused <scope> <key>: <why>` line in `<data>/ops/up.log`, and its job does not run; the rest of the render is written as usual.
Settings > Jobs lists an org's row in the shape of a job, an object whose heartbeat is its own name, before these checks, so a row they refuse still shows its switch, and its write is refused with the reason.
The job's `jobs.json` row names its org in `org`, so when its row is later refused or removed, the job stays listed as paused and the runner stops it, until a row of its name renders again.
A pass that cannot read the orgs at all keeps each org's job as the last `jobs.json` had it, and says so in `up.log`.

`config` is the job's own settings.
Playground checks only that it is an object, and writes it into the job's `jobs.json` row, where the job reads it and checks each field itself.
A registry job's row takes a `config` the same way.
